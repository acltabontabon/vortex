= ADR-039: Four limits, not one, and capacity names the one it found

== Status

Accepted, and implemented in slices 4.6 and 4.7 of
xref:../01-product/roadmap.adoc[Phase 4 — HTTP foundation hardening].

Three details were settled during implementation.

=== A ramp does not hold the level it arrives at

Condition 3 asks how long a level was *held*. A stage says "move to this target over this duration",
so a stage whose target differs from the one before it is a ramp: the level it names is where it
arrives, not where it stayed. Only a stage whose target matches its predecessor is a plateau, and
only plateaus are counted. Summing every stage that mentions a level would credit a ramp's whole
climb to the level it happened to end at — which is exactly the fifteen-seconds-versus-ten-minutes
confusion the condition exists to catch.

=== Headroom's old numerator is renamed, not overloaded

The two calculations take the same shaped arguments and differ only in their first parameter, so
adding the new one as an overload made every existing call ambiguous rather than broken — a compiler
error that says nothing about what changed. The sustainable-capacity calculation therefore keeps the
name `calculate`, and the historical one becomes `calculateFromTestedCapacity`. A caller wanting the
old numerator now has to ask for it by name.

Historical observations still use it. Rows recorded before this slice did not fail the five
conditions; nobody evaluated them, and retroactively refusing headroom on every past page would read
as a bug rather than as the announced discontinuity it is. `SustainableCapacity.notEvaluated()` and
"evaluated, not established" are deliberately different states for this reason, and `V4` stores the
conditions alongside the level so the difference survives a restart.

=== A capacity without resource telemetry is weaker, not absent

Condition 5 is `NOT_EVALUATED` where nothing with a declared limit covered the level. Refusing the
capacity outright would leave every team without resource telemetry with no figure at all; asserting
it as firmly as a fully observed run would overstate it. It is established at `MEDIUM` strength
instead, and the condition's own sentence says the question is unknown rather than ruled out.

== Context

Vortex already refuses to say "maximum throughput". It records a *tested SLO-compliant capacity*, the
level beside the first non-compliant level, both edges together, with a `BoundaryStatus` saying
whether they form a boundary at all — see
xref:adr-032-capacity-records-both-edges.adoc[ADR-032] and
xref:../03-performance-model/experiments.adoc[breakpoints and capacity].

It also distinguishes two limits: the `SloBreakpoint`, where an agreed objective was first violated,
and `SystemSaturation`, where the system itself appeared to stop coping. That second one requires two
independent signals and reports a bounded range.

Both are real and both are incomplete, in the same direction.

*The objective breakpoint is a statement about an agreement.* Raise p95 from 500 ms to 800 ms and it
moves while the service behaves identically. That is documented and correct, and it means the number
answers "when do we breach our SLO?" rather than "what can this service do?".

*System saturation is inferred from client-side symptoms.* Its three signals — rate shortfall, error
runaway, latency explosion — are all measured at the load generator. Two of them have an innocent
explanation that lives on the generator rather than the service: a shortfall is exactly what a
saturated generator produces, and a latency explosion measured client-side includes time the request
spent queued in a client that could not dispatch it. It is careful about corroboration and blind
about *whose* symptoms it is corroborating.

And neither is a resource statement. `ConstraintCandidate` records resources near their limits at the
boundary and says, in its own `describe()`, that coincidence is not causation. That is the right
epistemics and the wrong altitude: it can say a pool was at 98% where objectives broke, and it cannot
say the service reached a resource limit at all, because nothing in the domain knows which signals
are resources or what their limits are. ADR-037 fixes that; this ADR decides what to do with it.

Meanwhile the number a capacity page actually needs to answer — *what can this service sustain?* — is
not any of these. The highest level that passed is not a capacity if the generator produced it
badly, if it was held for forty seconds, or if throughput had already stopped responding to offered
load two stages earlier.

== Decision

=== Four limits, each with its own evidence and its own name

[cols="1,2,2", options="header"]
|===
| Limit | What it is | What it needs

| *Objective breakpoint*
| The level where a declared objective was first violated
| Thresholds and stages. Exists today as `SloBreakpoint`

| *Throughput ceiling*
| The level beyond which increasing offered load stops producing proportionally increased achieved
  throughput
| Offered and achieved rate per stage, and a run quality that rules out the generator (ADR-038)

| *Resource limit*
| The level where a typed resource signal reached its declared limit
| A `ResourceSignal` with a `ResourceLimit`, scoped to the system under test (ADR-037)

| *System saturation*
| The level where the system as a whole stopped coping, from corroborating symptoms
| Two independent signals. Exists today as `SystemSaturation`
|===

They are four different findings, reported separately, and they routinely disagree. A service can
breach a 200 ms p95 objective at 300 requests/sec while its throughput ceiling is 900 and its CPU
never passes 40% — that is a service that is slow, not a service that is full, and one number cannot
say both. Collapsing them into "the breakpoint" would throw away the distinction that tells an
engineer whether to tune the code, add instances, or renegotiate the objective.

*A throughput ceiling is not a shortfall.* The shape is `offered ↑, achieved flat, latency ↑` — a
queue forming somewhere. Where run quality says the generator was itself constrained, the same shape
is evidence about Vortex's host and is not reported as a property of the service at all.

*CPU is not privileged.* The resource limit is whichever typed signal reached its declared limit
first, and where none did, Vortex reports that no resource limit was observed. A run with CPU
telemetry and nothing else may not conclude CPU was the constraint merely because CPU is the only
thing it could see.

=== Sustainable capacity is demonstrated, not inferred

----
Sustainable capacity — the highest offered level for which all of the following hold:

  1. the offered load was actually generated          (run quality permits the claim)
  2. every declared objective was met                 (Verdict PASS at that level)
  3. it was held for the declared sustain duration    (not a ramp passing through)
  4. achieved throughput tracked offered load         (at or below the throughput ceiling)
  5. no observed resource reached its declared limit  (where such telemetry exists)
----

=== "Held" has a number behind it

Condition 3 is meaningless without one. A level a ramp passed through in fifteen seconds and a level
held for ten minutes are not the same evidence, and JIT compilation, connection-pool growth, cache
fill and the first collection cycle all land inside the first minute of any level.

Sustain duration is a property of the workload, defaulted by test type:

[cols="1,1,3", options="header"]
|===
| Test type | Default hold | Why

| `SMOKE`
| —
| Never quotable as capacity; it exists to check the workload is valid

| `AVERAGE_LOAD`
| 5m
| The run future releases are compared against. Long enough that warm-up is a small fraction of it

| `STRESS`, `BREAKPOINT`
| 2m per level
| The floor at which a stepped level is evidence rather than a transient. Ten levels at 5m each is a
  fifty-minute run nobody will schedule, and the boundary matters more than any single level

| `SPIKE`
| —
| Abrupt arrival is the subject; no level is held, and no capacity is claimed

| `SOAK`
| 30m
| Below this it is an average-load test with a longer name
|===

A level held for less than its sustain duration is still measured, still rendered, and cannot be a
sustainable-capacity edge. The finding says which number it missed and by how much.

Two minutes is a floor, not a recommendation. It is the point below which a level is measuring
warm-up; whether a given service needs five is a question its engineers answer, which is why the
value is declarable per workload rather than fixed.

Conditions 1–4 are computable from measurements Vortex either has or is adding in this phase.
Condition 5 is conditional on telemetry: where none exists, it is *not evaluated* rather than
assumed satisfied, and the resulting capacity is reported as such. A capacity established without
resource telemetry is a weaker claim than one established with it, and the difference is stated
rather than left to the reader.

Sustainable capacity will frequently be *lower* than today's tested SLO-compliant capacity, and will
sometimes not exist for a run that produces one. That is the point. The existing figure keeps its
name and its meaning — it is the highest compliant edge, and it remains the honest answer to "what
level passed?". Sustainable capacity answers a stricter question and says so.

=== Sustainable capacity is the headline, and headroom is computed from it

A capacity result's most common real use is a deployment decision — somebody has to say whether this
release can carry production traffic. That reader needs the defensible number in the largest type,
not the most flattering one.

----
Sustainable capacity      410 requests/sec
Production peak           120 requests/sec
Headroom                  3.4×

Highest level that passed 450 requests/sec
                          not sustainable: achieved throughput flattened above 410
----

Both figures always appear. The headline is sustainable capacity; tested SLO-compliant capacity sits
beneath it as *highest level that passed*, which is what it has always been.

Where sustainable capacity does not exist, the headline is the refusal and the unmet condition, and
the highest passing level is shown below it explicitly labelled as not a capacity claim. That is the
mechanism `HeadroomCalculator` already uses, applied one level higher.

*Headroom's numerator becomes sustainable capacity.* This is a deliberate change to an existing
figure and the whole reason the figure exists: headroom is the number that ends up on a slide
justifying a deployment, and dividing production traffic into a level the service was never
demonstrated to sustain is precisely the arithmetic that makes such a slide wrong. Every existing
refusal stays — isolated tests, absent production observation, mismatched units, unstable boundary —
and joins a fifth: no sustainable capacity was established, and which condition failed.

=== The limiting signal is named only when a signal named itself

A capacity conclusion carries a *first limiting signal*: which of the four limits was reached first,
and, where the answer is a resource limit, which typed resource. It is stated only where the
evidence is present. Where two limits were reached at the same level, both are named. Where the
available telemetry cannot identify one:

[quote]
____
No resource limit can be identified from the available telemetry. Objectives were first violated at
450 requests/sec; CPU and memory were observed and neither reached a declared limit; no network,
disk or runtime-pause telemetry was collected.
____

That sentence is a better result than a confident guess, and it doubles as instructions for the next
run.

=== Efficiency is a comparison metric, and only in the units it was measured in

Normalised efficiency — CPU-seconds per request, bytes of memory per unit of throughput — is
defensible only when the numerator is scoped to the system under test, measured over the same window
as the denominator, and expressed as an absolute quantity rather than a percentage. "42% CPU at 300
requests/sec" versus "74% CPU at 300 requests/sec" is a meaningful comparison only if both figures
came from the same core count on the same shape of host; a percentage of an unstated denominator is
not a metric, it is a coincidence.

So efficiency figures are computed and reported *within a comparison of compatible experiments*, never
as a standalone number on a single run's page, and they carry the `DERIVED` source that already marks
values Vortex calculated rather than measured.

== Consequences

*Gained.* A capacity conclusion states what stopped the service, or states that nothing observed did.
The four limits stop being one overloaded word, and the difference between "slow" and "full" survives
into the report. Sustainable capacity is a figure that can be defended in a review, because every one
of its five conditions is separately checkable and separately falsifiable.

*Cost.* Five conditions is five ways for a run not to produce a capacity, and the most common early
outcome will be "no sustainable capacity was established" on runs that today confidently report one.
Headroom moves with the numerator, so figures already recorded against tested compliant capacity are
not comparable with figures recorded after this change — a discontinuity in a number teams may
already be tracking, and one worth announcing rather than shipping quietly. Four limits mean four things to render on a page
that is already dense, and the interface work to keep that legible is not small. Efficiency being
comparison-only will disappoint the first person who wants it on a single run.

*Trade accepted.* The alternative is one number labelled "capacity", which is what makes performance
results quotable and what makes them wrong. Vortex has already accepted this trade twice — refusing
headroom rather than computing it from an isolated test, refusing a boundary that was not monotonic —
and both refusals turned out to be the parts of the product worth trusting. A capacity figure that
frequently declines to exist is the cost of one that means something when it does.
