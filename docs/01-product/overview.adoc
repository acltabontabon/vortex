== Vision

=== The sentence

Vortex is a local-first performance engineering workbench: it helps engineers model realistic
workloads, execute repeatable tests against a service, understand its capacity, detect regressions
and diagnose bottlenecks — without every engineer having to become a performance-testing specialist.
It builds on k6 for load generation and an optional local Ollama model for interpretation, never the
other way around.

=== The experience we are building towards

[quote]
____
An engineer takes a microservice with no performance tests and establishes a repeatable,
understandable, production-informed performance baseline in less than one working day.
____

Not a load test. A _baseline_: a number they can defend, with the conditions it was measured under
attached, that will still mean something when someone re-runs it next quarter.

=== Why this is not a load-testing tool

Load generation is solved. k6 is excellent, it is open source, and rebuilding any part of it would
be a waste of everyone's time.

What is not solved is everything around it. A team with k6 installed still has to decide what
workload represents their traffic, how much of it to generate, what a reasonable threshold is, which
of the numbers in the output matter, whether this week's result is worse than last week's, and what
to do about it. Those decisions require judgement that most teams have to reinvent, badly, one
service at a time.

Vortex is the accumulated answer to those questions, made into a tool.

----
                     VORTEX
                        │
          ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
          ▼             ▼             ▼

      MODELLING      EXECUTION    UNDERSTANDING

      Workloads         k6        Deterministic metrics
      Traffic mixes     │         Capacity
      Workloads         │         Breakpoints
      Thresholds        │         Regression
      Calibration       │         Interpretation

                        │
                        ▼

               PERFORMANCE EVIDENCE
----

k6 generates load. Ollama helps interpret evidence. Docker provides a local laboratory. Observability
systems provide telemetry. Vortex connects them into a workflow that produces something a team can
act on.

=== What "good" looks like

Vortex succeeds when a problem that felt vague becomes tractable:

[cols="1,1", options="header"]
|===
| Before
| After

| "Can it handle Black Friday?"
| "It met its objectives at 212 requests/sec in the performance environment with real dependencies, against an observed peak of 120. That is 1.76× headroom."

| "The test failed, I think?"
| "p95 exceeded 500 ms first at 212 requests/sec, and status polling degraded before submission did. The connection pool was saturated at the same time. CPU was not."

| "Is this slower than last release?"
| "Yes: p95 at the same rate, mix and environment moved from 280 ms to 405 ms."

| "Where do I even start?"
| "Import your OpenAPI document."
|===

=== What we will not trade away

*Trustworthiness over capability.* A number Vortex reports must be defensible. Where the evidence
does not support a conclusion, Vortex says so — "not established by this test" is a feature.

*Honesty over polish.* A result carries its conditions. A local run against mocked dependencies is
labelled as such wherever its numbers appear, because the alternative is somebody putting
"1000 TPS" on a slide.

*Determinism over intelligence.* Verdicts, breakpoints, headroom and regressions are computed by
ordinary code. A language model that produced them would make the same data yield different answers
on different days, and everything downstream would inherit that.

*Local-first over hosted.* No account, no cluster, no server, no SaaS. An engineer should be able
to learn performance testing on their laptop during an afternoon.

=== What success is not

Not feature count. Vortex should be impressive because an engineering problem that previously felt
vague suddenly feels straightforward — not because it has hundreds of features.

== Problem statement

=== The observation

Teams that need performance testing usually have access to excellent performance-testing tools and
do not use them. When they do use them, the results frequently do not survive contact with a
sceptical reader.

The gap is not technology. It is that performance testing requires a chain of judgements, each of
which is easy to get wrong, and getting any of them wrong quietly invalidates everything after it.

=== The chain

Ask a team to performance-test a service and they must decide, in order:

. *What traffic should we model?* Most teams do not know their own numbers. Someone picks 100
  requests per second because it is round.
. *What kind of test is this?* Baseline, peak, stress, spike, soak and capacity answer different
  questions. Choosing the wrong one produces an answer to a question nobody asked.
. *What traffic are we generating?* Blasting one endpoint tells you about that endpoint. Production
  sends a mixture of concurrent requests across many endpoints, and services fall over on the mix.
. *How many virtual users?* A question with no good answer, which changes every time latency
  does, and which silently reduces the offered load exactly when the service starts to struggle.
. *What threshold?* Without one, the run produces measurements and no verdict.
. *What do these numbers mean?* A load generator's output is a wall of metrics. Which one
  indicates a problem is not obvious to someone doing this for the first time.
. *Where was the bottleneck?* "p95 = 800 ms" describes the symptom. The useful question is what
  ran out.
. *Is this repeatable?* A test that lives in one person's terminal history is not.
. *Is this worse than last time?* Only answerable if the two runs tested the same thing, which is
  only knowable if that was recorded.
. *Is it safe to run?* Load tests cause incidents.

Each link is where a team stalls, and there is no tool whose job is the chain.

=== What goes wrong in practice

*Invented traffic.* A number chosen because it sounded plausible. The test then measures the
service's response to a fiction.

*Endpoint blasting.* 500 requests/sec at one path, passing comfortably, while the same service under
its real mixture of traffic would have failed at 200 — because the expensive operation nobody tested
was contending for the same pool.

*Conflated units.* "50" meaning requests per second in one sentence and virtual users in the next.
They are not the same quantity, and no conversion exists without the latency the test was supposed to
find out. The error survives into a capacity plan.

*Context-free capacity.* "We handle 1000 TPS" on a slide. Measured locally. Against mocks.

*Green tests that measured nothing.* An objective whose measurement was never collected, reported
as a pass.

*Unrepeatable results.* A number nobody can reproduce, from a configuration nobody kept.

*Confident wrong diagnoses.* A bottleneck identified from telemetry that was never gathered.

=== What Vortex changes

Vortex takes the chain seriously as the product:

[cols="2,3", options="header"]
|===
| The judgement
| What Vortex does

| What traffic?
| Records observed production traffic and derives workloads from it by documented arithmetic

| What kind of test?
| Asks what you want to find out, in your own terms, and maps it to an executor

| What traffic?
| A workload reproduces a realistic operation mix; one operation is also a complete target

| How many virtual users?
| You never say. You state a throughput; Vortex handles the rest

| What threshold?
| Suggests a starting point, says plainly it is not a standard, evaluates deterministically

| What do the numbers mean?
| Opens with the question you asked and answers it in a sentence

| Where was the bottleneck?
| Correlates client-side measurements with the service's own telemetry, and says when it cannot

| Repeatable?
| The definition is a file in your repository; every run snapshots its resolved plan

| Worse than last time?
| Compares freely, but only issues a regression verdict when the runs are equivalent

| Safe?
| Shows exactly what will happen, classifies the environment, and requires typed confirmation where it matters
|===

Teams do not need a better load generator. They need the workflow around one.

== Principles

These are the rules Vortex holds itself to. Where a feature would violate one, the feature loses.

'''

=== 1. Production-informed over arbitrary

Prefer real workload evidence to invented traffic numbers. A test against a number someone picked
because it sounded plausible measures the service's response to a fiction.

_In practice:_ observed production traffic is a first-class input, and workloads derived from it
record how the number was arrived at.

=== 2. Realistic composition over endpoint blasting

Production systems experience a mixture of concurrent traffic across many endpoints, not isolated
benchmarks. A service that comfortably serves 500 lookups a second may fall over at 50 checkouts.

_In practice:_ a workload reproduces a weighted operation mix, and that is ordinary rather than
advanced. One operation is also a complete target — the simple case stays simple.

=== 3. Arrival rate over guessed virtual users

Throughput is what service owners know. Virtual-user counts are an artefact of the load generator
that must be guessed, and a virtual-user test quietly reduces its own offered load exactly when the
service starts to struggle.

_In practice:_ users state requests per second, and arrival rate is the default. Concurrency is
available when the real caller is a bounded population, and is never confused with a rate.

=== 4. Evidence over opinion

Performance conclusions must be grounded in measurements. Every claim traces to something measured,
and it is always clear which claims are measurements, which are calculations, and which are
interpretations.

_In practice:_ AI findings cite evidence by identifier, and unresolvable citations are discarded.
Recommendations carry the same requirement, so generic advice is discarded along with unsupported
findings rather than surviving because it sounds reasonable.

=== 5. AI assists; deterministic systems decide

Never delegate truth to a language model. Measurements, verdicts, breakpoints, headroom and
regressions are computed by ordinary code.

_In practice:_ Vortex calculates that 67 × 1.5 = 100.5 and rounds it by a documented rule. The model
explains what that means. It is never asked to multiply.

=== 6. Local first

An engineer should be able to learn performance testing, validate a workload and catch a regression
on their own machine. No account, no cluster, no server, no SaaS.

_In practice:_ Vortex plus k6 plus the service under test is enough. Everything else is optional.

=== 7. Environment context matters

Local mocks do not equal production capacity. What a result can legitimately claim depends on where
it was measured.

_In practice:_ every result, report and capacity figure carries its environment classification.

=== 8. Progressive disclosure

Keep beginners productive without restricting experts. Someone who owns a service can answer "can it
handle expected peak traffic?"; nobody can answer "constant-arrival-rate or ramping?" without first
learning a load generator.

_In practice:_ intent-first questions, with the generated configuration always inspectable.

=== 9. Safe by default

Performance tests can cause incidents. Vortex does not assume a user intends to flood whatever URL
appears in a config file.

_In practice:_ every run shows its target and environment class first; non-local targets require the
environment's name to be typed; rates have per-environment ceilings; mutating operations need
explicit review.

=== 10. Reproducibility matters

A performance claim should link back to its configuration and evidence.

_In practice:_ the definition lives in the service's repository; every run snapshots the fully
resolved plan, the generated workload, the raw output and the tool versions.

=== 11. Adopt before building

Use existing open-source tools where they already solve the problem well.

_In practice:_ k6 generates load. Ollama runs models. Docker runs dependencies. Vortex owns the
workflow.

=== 12. Modular monolith first

Do not distribute an architecture before the problem requires it. Microservices for a local
developer tool would be architectural cosplay.

_In practice:_ one process, one binary, module boundaries that quarantine real dependencies.

=== 13. A performance test is an experiment

Not "send traffic, get number", but:

----
Hypothesis → Controlled workload → Known environment → Measured observations → Evaluation → Conclusion
----

Every test plan should answer: _what are we trying to learn?_

_In practice:_ plans carry an explicit intent, and the result page opens by returning to the
question rather than by showing a chart.

=== Deliberate non-goals

==== A replacement for k6
We use k6. Vortex does not implement HTTP load generation, virtual users, arrival-rate scheduling or
distributed execution, and adding any of it would be a mistake.

==== A replacement for k6 Studio
Grafana's https://grafana.com/docs/k6-studio/[k6 Studio] already does browser and proxy recording,
HAR import and visual script authoring, and does it well. Vortex's value begins above generic script
creation: record a complex flow in k6 Studio, import the script into Vortex, attach workload
metadata and thresholds, execute, analyse and compare.

==== An observability platform
Vortex consumes telemetry. It does not store time series, run a query language, or replace Grafana,
Prometheus, Dynatrace or anything similar.

==== An APM product
Different problem, different shape, vastly more scope.

==== A Kubernetes platform
Remote and distributed execution (see xref:roadmap.adoc[the roadmap]) will eventually run through a
Vortex Coordinator that talks to the Kubernetes API to create short-lived Jobs for a Vortex Runner.
Vortex will not become a general Kubernetes scheduler, and the Coordinator is never part of local
execution — Vortex plus k6 plus the service under test remains enough on its own.

==== A generic AI chatbot
The assistant supports specific performance-engineering workflows. There is no agent loop, no
memory, no vector store and no retrieval layer, because none of them solve the problem at hand.

==== A traffic proxy or production recorder
Not in this MVP. This means capturing and replaying real production traffic. A future capability that
reproduces the observed _statistical characteristics_ of a dependency's latency and failures during a
local run (see xref:roadmap.adoc[the roadmap]) is a different thing — it never records or replays
actual traffic, only a distribution derived from it.

==== A chaos-engineering platform
Chaos Mesh and Litmus exist. Integration may make sense later; replacement does not. Reproducing a
dependency's _observed_ production behaviour for fidelity (xref:roadmap.adoc[the roadmap]) is adjacent
but distinct from fault injection for resilience testing — a dependency profile replays what was
measured, not an arbitrary failure chosen to see what breaks.

==== A synthetic monitoring platform
Continuous availability monitoring is a different problem.

==== A guarantee of production capacity
This one matters most. A test provides *evidence under documented conditions*. Vortex is built to
make those conditions inseparable from the number, and its language reflects it: _tested
SLO-compliant capacity_, never _guaranteed maximum throughput_.

==== An autonomous performance engineer
AI proposes; humans decide. Vortex will not start a test on its own, and there is no configuration
that makes it.

==== An unrestricted traffic generator
Safety policies are not optional. Vortex is an engineering tool for investigating your own systems.

==== A messaging platform
Asynchronous workload testing (see the xref:roadmap.adoc[roadmap]) publishes messages at a controlled
rate in service of a capacity question, the same way k6 does for HTTP — it is not Postman for queues,
an AWS console replacement, a Kafka management UI, or a generic message-publishing utility. Supporting
one broker does not make Vortex a broker client.

==== A document designer
Three export formats — JSON, Markdown, PDF — chosen because each answers a different question about
who is reading and where. There is no report designer, no template language, no drag-and-drop layout
and no chart builder. A fourth format needs a reason, not a plugin point, and the first request to
put a logo on the PDF is answered with an ADR rather than a theming system.

=== Boundaries we expect to move

Documented as future direction, not stubbed out:

* Remote execution on a Vortex Coordinator, then distributed execution across several ephemeral
  workers — Kubernetes/EKS first
* CI templates and performance gates
* Scheduled runs
* Organisational policy packs
* Asynchronous workload testing — AWS SQS first, Kafka and JMS as deliberate future extensions
* Production-informed dependency simulation — reproducing observed production dependency latency and
  failure characteristics during local runs, increasing an isolated test's fidelity without changing
  its classification

Two that have since moved: Prometheus and Dynatrace are now supported both as production observation
sources and as during-run observability providers, and traffic can be calibrated from one rather than
typed in by hand. Capacity history per service version is recorded. See the
xref:roadmap.adoc[roadmap].

== Terminology

Terms Vortex uses, and what it means by them. Where a word is commonly used loosely, the loose usage
is named too, because that is usually where confusion starts.

=== The model in seven words

|===
| *Service* | what system?
| *Operation* | what interaction?
| *Workload* | what traffic?
| *Evaluation* | what question?
| *Quality gate* | what is acceptable?
| *Run* | what execution?
| *Evidence* | what happened?

=== Current vocabulary

==== Levels and rates

*Arrival rate* — how many requests are offered per second, on a schedule, independently of how the
service responds. The control quantity of an _open_ workload. Written `requests/sec`.

*Concurrency* — how many virtual users are active, each waiting for its previous request before
issuing the next. The control quantity of a _closed_ workload. Written `VUs`.

*Achieved throughput* — how many requests per second the service actually took. An _observed_
quantity. Under an open workload the gap between offered and achieved is a signal; under a closed
workload throughput is an outcome and there is nothing it fell short of.

*Load level* — whichever of the two a given workload controls. Vortex never displays one without its
unit: `50 requests/sec` and `50 VUs` are the same number and different facts, and no conversion
between them exists without a latency. See
xref:../03-performance-model/workloads.adoc[arrival rate vs concurrency].

*Operation rate* — how often one specific operation is invoked per second. One operation is one
request, so an operation's arrival rate and its request rate are the same figure.

*TPS* — "transactions per second". Vortex avoids this term. It is ambiguous unless the transaction
boundary is stated in the same sentence, and that ambiguity is a reliable source of factor-of-three
errors.

==== Latency

*p50, p95, p99* — percentiles. p95 = 500 ms means 95% of requests completed within 500 ms.
Percentiles rather than averages, because an average hides the unluckiest users: a service with a
90 ms mean can still be failing one request in twenty at three seconds.

*Error rate* — the share of requests that failed, as a percentage.

==== The system under test

*System under test (SUT)* — the one deployable service a run is evaluating. Typically a microservice
representing a bounded capability.

*Dependency* — a database, downstream service or broker the SUT calls. Dependencies certainly affect
the numbers; they are not additional systems under test. What they change is what a result may claim,
which Vortex models through the environment's _dependency mode_ and _classification_.

*Operation* — one meaningful interaction with the SUT: a method, a path, its parameters and body. A
machine-discoverable fact from an API description. *A single operation is a complete performance
target* — nothing else is needed to test one.

*Operation binding* — what a person decided about an operation: request data, expected response, and
whether a mutating operation has been approved. Kept separate from the operation itself so
re-importing a specification never discards it.

*Request data* — what a request actually carries: its headers, path and query parameters, and body.
Answers _what gets sent_, where a workload answers _how much traffic_. Belongs to the operation, so
every workload that exercises it inherits the same values.

*Value source* — where one request value comes from. Four of them: *fixed* (a literal), *generated*
(Vortex produces it), *dataset* (a field of the current row of a file you added), and *environment*
(read from a variable when the load generator starts, and nowhere else). The set is closed on
purpose — see xref:../adr/adr-036-request-data-is-declared-not-scripted.adoc[ADR-036].

*Dataset* — a CSV or JSON file of realistic values a request can draw from. Rows are used in order
and wrap at the end; every value one request reads from a dataset comes from the same row. A dataset
is either _local_ (held on one machine) or _portable_ (committed with the service).

*Generation lifecycle* — how often a generated value is produced: a new one for every request, or one
per virtual user. Not decoration: an idempotency key generated once per virtual user makes every
request after the first a duplicate submission.

==== Workloads

*Workload* — the workload Vortex intends to reproduce against the SUT: a test type, an operation mix,
a workload model and shape, a duration, and the objectives that must hold. Reusable, and it lives in
`vortex.yaml`. Not a business journey, and not a k6 scenario.

*Workload model* — _open_ (arrival rate) or _closed_ (concurrency). The oldest distinction in load
modelling, and the one that decides what a test is measuring.

*Operation mix* — how a workload's traffic is composed across operations, as relative weights.
`70/30` and `7/3` mean the same thing. It describes _aggregate concurrent composition_, not a
sequence — see xref:../03-performance-model/workloads.adoc[composition vs sequence]. Meaningful only
under an open workload, where the weights genuinely divide traffic.

*Workload source* — where a workload's numbers came from: observed in production, derived from an
observation by a stated policy, or manually entered. Shown beside the figure, because a conclusion
inherits the confidence of its weakest input.

*Stage* — one step of a ramping workload, and one observation of "did the service still meet its
objectives at this level?".

===== Kinds of test

[cols="1,3", options="header"]
|===
| Kind
| The question it answers

| *Smoke*
| Is the workload valid and can Vortex reach the service?

| *Average load*
| Does the service meet its objectives under the traffic it normally receives?

| *Stress*
| How does it behave at or above its expected peak?

| *Spike*
| How does it react to a sudden jump in demand?

| *Soak*
| Does performance degrade during sustained load?

| *Breakpoint*
| At what level does it stop meeting its objectives?
|===

A test type is *intent*. It selects no executor and constrains no workload shape: a soak may be
closed-model, a spike may be a step change in either quantity. Vortex guides the pairing and refuses
none of it.

Two words deliberately absent from this list. *Baseline* is a comparison concept — the run you
measure against — not a kind of workload; Vortex records it as a capacity observation and a plan
fingerprint. *Volume* describes the size of the data the service holds rather than the traffic
arriving at it, and belongs with the environment a run was executed against.

==== Results

*Threshold / objective* — what the service is expected to achieve. Turns measurements into a
verdict.

*Pass / Fail / Not evaluated* — _Not evaluated_ means a measurement an objective needed was not
collected. It is never reported as a pass: an objective that was never checked has not been met.

*SLO breakpoint* — the level at which an objective was _first_ violated, stated with its unit.
Deterministic. A statement about an agreement, not about the machine — raise the objective and it
moves while the service behaves identically.

*System breakpoint / saturation* — where the system itself stopped coping: runaway errors, inability
to sustain the offered load, resource exhaustion. Contextual and noisy, so Vortex reports
a bounded range backed by several independent signals, or says *not established*. That is
frequently the correct answer, and it is a feature.

*Highest compliant level* — the highest tested level at which every objective was still met.

*Evidence strength* — how well supported a finding is, given what was actually collected. Applies
to deterministic findings, not only AI ones: a breakpoint from twelve stages is firmer than one from
two, even though the arithmetic is identical.

==== Capacity

*Tested SLO-compliant capacity* — the highest level at which the service met every objective, _under
the recorded conditions_. Vortex never says "capacity" unqualified.

*Capacity observation* — that figure together with its conditions: version, environment,
classification, dependency mode, workload model, operation mix, objectives, duration, execution.
Called an _observation_ rather than a _baseline_ because tested capacity is not a property a service
has — it moves with the version, the configuration, the infrastructure and the size of the data.

*Headroom* — tested compliant capacity ÷ observed production peak. Only computed when the two are
comparable, which among other things means both are request rates: virtual users and requests per
second are not divisible.

*Observed production traffic* — what the service actually receives: average, p95, peak, and *how that
traffic is composed across operations*. What makes a workload production-informed rather than
invented. The composition matters as much as the volume — 120 requests/sec of status polling is a
different workload from 120 requests/sec of submissions.

==== Environments

*Isolated performance test* — dependencies simulated or controlled. Good evidence for application
bottlenecks, concurrency, connection pools and regressions. *Does not establish production
capacity.*

*Integrated performance test* — real dependencies, production-like infrastructure. Good evidence
for realistic capacity, dependency latency and platform limits.

*Dependency mode* — whether downstream systems are real, mocked or mixed.

A dependency simulated from observed production characteristics is still simulated: it does not move
a test from isolated to integrated, however faithful the simulation. See
xref:roadmap.adoc[production-informed dependency simulation].

==== Execution

*Effective test plan* — the fully resolved description of what will run: defaults applied,
environment resolved, per-operation rates allocated, k6 scenario keys assigned, runner chosen, safety
decisions recorded. Snapshot per execution; the fundamental unit of reproducibility.

*Run* — one immutable execution of a workload. Workloads are reusable; runs are history.

*Plan fingerprint* — a content hash of the normalised plan. Two runs with the same fingerprint
tested the same thing.

*Artifact* — raw evidence kept on disk: the plan, the generated workload, the engine summary, the
sample stream, the output logs, the report.

==== Analysis

*Measured / Calculated / AI-interpreted* — the three tiers Vortex keeps visibly distinct.
Measurements come from the engine or the service; calculations from deterministic code;
interpretations from a language model, always labelled and always citing measurements that exist.

*Evidence reference* — the identifier by which a finding cites a measurement, e.g.
`metric:checkout.pool.pending`, `threshold:latency.p95`, or, for a comparison, `delta:latency.p95`.
Unresolvable references are discarded before display.

*Finding type* — `OBSERVATION` / `CORRELATION` / `HYPOTHESIS` / `LIMITATION`: what kind of claim an
AI finding is making, distinct from *Evidence strength* above (which grades a deterministic
breakpoint) and from *Finding level* (`fail`/`warning`/`observation`/`pass`, which grades a
deterministic finding's severity). A finding's type bounds how much confidence it may claim — a
hypothesis may never claim `HIGH` merely because two figures moved together.

*Missing telemetry* — a measurement that would have helped but was not collected. Stated
explicitly, because "Vortex cannot identify the saturation point because connection-pool telemetry
was not collected" is far more useful than a confident guess.

=== Vocabulary reserved for Phase 4

These terms describe a stricter, resource-aware notion of capacity that Vortex does not yet compute.
They are defined now so that when they do appear, they are recognised rather than mistaken for
today's `tested SLO-compliant capacity`. See xref:roadmap.adoc[] for the phase they belong to.

*Sustainable capacity* — the highest offered level that was actually generated as offered, met every
objective, was held rather than passed through, tracked offered load, and reached no observed
resource limit. Stricter than tested SLO-compliant capacity, and frequently lower or absent. Planned;
not implemented today. See xref:roadmap.adoc[].

*Throughput ceiling* — the level beyond which increasing offered load stops producing proportionally
increased achieved throughput. A property of the system, not of an agreement. Planned; not
implemented today. See xref:roadmap.adoc[].

*Resource limit* — the level at which an observed resource reached the constraint it is measured
against. Distinct from system saturation, which is inferred from client-side symptoms. Planned; not
implemented today. See xref:roadmap.adoc[].

*First limiting signal* — which of the four limits a run reached first, and where it was a resource
limit, which resource. Stated only where evidence identifies one. Planned; not implemented today.
See xref:roadmap.adoc[].

*Run quality* — whether the experiment was carried out as specified: valid, degraded or invalid,
with reason codes citing measurements. Separate from the verdict, which says whether the service met
its objectives. Planned; not implemented today. See xref:roadmap.adoc[].

*Resource signal* — a measurement classified by what kind of resource it describes (CPU, memory,
network, disk, runtime memory, runtime pause, pool, queue, threads), whose system it describes
(system under test, load generator, dependency), and the limit it is relative to. Planned; not
implemented today. See xref:roadmap.adoc[].

=== Vocabulary reserved for Phase 8

Terms for a capability the roadmap describes but Vortex does not yet build. Defined now for the same
reason as the terms above: so they are recognised later rather than reinvented differently.

*Declared dependency* — a dependency a service has explicitly named as materially participating in its
workload, distinct from every downstream call the SUT happens to make. Carries a production identity,
a local target, and the production observation source Vortex should ask about it. Planned; not
implemented today. See xref:roadmap.adoc[].

*Dependency profile* — a reusable description of a declared dependency's observed production

*Production-informed dependency simulation* — reproducing a dependency profile during a local run so
the service under test experiences realistic latency, jitter, timeouts and failures from its
dependencies, without requiring the real ones. Increases the _fidelity_ of an isolated test; does not
change its classification. Planned; not implemented today. See xref:roadmap.adoc[].

*Simulation plan* — the resolved mapping from a service's declared dependencies to the dependency
profiles and simulation configuration a run will actually apply — the dependency-side counterpart to
the effective test plan. Planned; not implemented today. See xref:roadmap.adoc[].
